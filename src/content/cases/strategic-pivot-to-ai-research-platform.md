## A search platform built on graph ontologies

I was responsible for researching and designing many features on our AI research platform and some have their own case studies linked throughout this text. This case study is about the bigger decision behind them, why we pivoted, how I made the case for it, and what it taught me.

I joined KiteEdge as a UX consultant in 2018 to help deliver Apex, our first MVP. Apex was a research search platform built on graph ontologies, designed to break down data silos and surface relationships between research that analysts hadn't spotted. However, Apex wasn't getting the traction we needed, development was too slow, and we were running out of runway.



## A strategic Pivot

 Interviews and conversations with experts showed that investment firms felt they had to adopt AI or be left behind, but didn't know where to start. They were worried about losing their IP to public AI, and didn't have the in-house expertise to build their own. That was our opening: an AI research assistant built on what Apex had taught us.

We kept the design and the ideas, including paragraph-level citations, and dropped the code. Angular's frequent major releases brought breaking changes and constant upgrade work, which took time a team our size couldn't spare. Our senior developer recommended moving to Blazor, so the same developers could share front-end and back-end work, and I backed it.

Letting go of five years of development was a hard decision. I made the case to our CEO that our investment hadn't been wasted, because it had brought us to the point where we could get ahead of the AI wave. It took a lot of convincing. Ultimately, the decision was his, and it was a judgement call rather than a proven case. Through the pivot and the uncertainty that came with it, I was asked to take on the CPO/CXO role.

We built Contours conversation-first, tackling hallucination before search. It was a risk, because analysts weren't used to working that way, but we felt it would set us apart.

## Ideas from Apex, built for AI

Many of the ideas behind Contours came from Apex, including scope, paragraph-level citations, collections, licensed content with organisation, team and user permissions, and an expert network that would find internal experts from usage data. Not all of them were built, but they carried forward on the roadmap. Citation and verification, for example, let analysts move from an AI-generated claim to the supporting passage in the original document.

We wanted AI to work as a trusted research assistant, not an authoritative answer machine. Professionals needed to see what information the AI could use, where an answer came from and what evidence supported it. We captured this in a model we called Define → Reason → Commit → Act.

## Letting current needs drive the backlog

We set out to let current user needs drive the backlog, not Apex's old plans. Multi-modal deep research (MMDR) came from a prospective client's need, and wouldn't have been imaginable in 2018. An independent research provider's senior researchers spent significant time turning existing research into weekly and monthly client bulletins. MMDR reproduced their structure and tone with full citation, reducing that work to minutes. Their first response was: "If you can solve this problem, you have a sale." The proof of concept didn't go ahead, because the provider worried clients would value their research less if AI was involved. Technical capability wasn't enough on its own; adoption depended on trust and professional identity.

As AI sped up development, I set up a workflow that kept design and code in step without design holding up the team. → Read more: Adopting AI-native workflows: keeping design and code in step

## From concept to commercial use

Contours went from concept to commercial use with professional investment teams, with 50+ active users, 10,000+ documents and 60 research providers. MMDR later led to a second proof of concept, producing thematic reports from PubMed data. I stayed hands-on throughout, from mapping workflows and designing in Figma to building the design system and working React interfaces with Claude Code.

## What held us back, and what it taught me

It was the right call. The pivot gave us an MVP we could actually release; without it, I believe we would have failed trying to make another sale with Apex. The move to Blazor paid off too: we ended up with five C# developers who could all cover for each other, and development was quicker. That pace pushed me to implement new design-to-code approaches to keep up.

What held us back was a tension between building a focused tool for a specific user need and the pull towards a bigger vision aimed at investors: decision intelligence and a governance layer. That vision took much of my time and energy. I pushed for features that each solved a clear need, because they showed who we were for and what we were selling. MMDR was the clearest case. But the vision, and the next feature that would make us ready, often won out, and I think that cost us.

### What it taught me:

- A small team can never out-build the big players, so it has to win through specialism. When OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Financial Services, it could have felt like the end of our ambitions. We read it as validation instead. Rather than building a specialist tool, OpenAI had packaged ChatGPT with built-in financial data and ready-made connectors.
- Research for small, agile teams works best when it's continuous and just enough.
- Decisions and leadership need to be backed by evidence; otherwise they come down to who argues loudest. I don't talk over people. I'd rather let the evidence speak.
